# Membership entitlement derives from a paid-through date, and Stripe is refetched rather than trusted

A visitor's paid-content access is decided by a **paid-through date** on the Visitor profile, extended during a payment failure by an explicit **dunning grace**, and never by the mirrored Stripe subscription status. Every webhook that concerns a subscription is treated as a bare trigger: the handler refetches the subscription from the Stripe API and derives all profile state from that one response, rather than reading the payload Stripe delivered.

The status enum was doing the entitlement's job, and it was wrong in the ordinary case. `invoice.payment_failed` set `subscriptionStatus` to `past_due` on the first declined renewal, and the entitlement function honoured a remaining paid period only for `cancelled` and `none` — so a visitor whose card expired lost access on day one while Stripe was still smart-retrying that card for roughly three weeks, and the account page, which has no `past_due` branch at all, showed them as a free member with an upgrade button they could use to buy a second subscription. Worse, the same handler nulled both date columns whenever status left `active`, so by the time anyone wanted to be generous there was no record left of what the visitor had paid for. Entitlement now reads one question — is the paid-through date in the future — and dunning cannot answer it. `CONTEXT.md` had already drawn this line, listing "subscription status" as a term to avoid for entitlement; the code had simply drifted from it.

The paid-through date is deliberately **not** Stripe's `current_period_end`, and the fixture capture is what forced that distinction. Stripe advances the period at the renewal moment, before the renewal charge clears: in the captured run the period jumped to 10-14 while that invoice was still unpaid, and stayed 10-14 after the charge failed. Treating `current_period_end` as paid-through would therefore hand a visitor who paid nothing a full month of access, and would hand it indefinitely if the account's retry policy ends by marking subscriptions `unpaid` rather than cancelling them. `current_period_end` is the period the visitor is *in*; those two values coincide only while payments succeed. So paid-through advances only when a period is actually paid, and access during a failure comes from a separate bounded **dunning grace** measured from the failed renewal — visible in the data, and incapable of quietly becoming a free month.

The grace is **five days**, and the length was chosen from the capture rather than from taste. In the recorded run the charge failed at 09-15 13:10 and Stripe scheduled its first retry for 09-19 20:26 — roughly four days and seven hours later. Any grace shorter than that revokes access before Stripe has retried the card even once, which would lock out precisely the visitors the retry is going to recover and reproduce a milder version of the bug being fixed. Five days clears that first attempt with a few hours to spare while costing a genuine non-payer only five days of a month they did not pay for. A shorter window was asked for and rejected on this evidence; a longer one buys the second retry too, at the price of more unpaid access.

`subscriptionRenewsAt` and `membershipExpiration` were always the same Stripe value, `current_period_end`, stored under two names chosen by what was expected to happen next. That framing is why nobody populated either during dunning: one was named for renewal and the other documented as being "for a cancelled subscription". They collapse into **`paidThroughAt`**, always populated while a subscription exists and never cleared by a status change, with meaning derived at the read site — the access-end date when `cancelAtPeriodEnd` is set, the next-charge date when it is not, the grace deadline while retries run. The collapse ships in two migrations, the additive backfill first and the destructive drop as its own later change, because this is a live database.

Refetching instead of reading the delivered payload is the part that will look wasteful to a future reader, since the event already contains a serialized subscription. It is deliberate. Webhook payloads are rendered at the endpoint's pinned API version while the SDK pins its own, and those versions disagree on this account by a margin of five years: captured events render at `2020-08-27`, where `current_period_end` sits at the subscription root, while the SDK pins `2025-08-27.basil`, where it exists only on subscription items. `handleSubscriptionDeleted` read the root field with no fallback, so its paid-period honouring is dead code against any SDK-shaped object and survives today only because the account's default version is stale — invisibly, because the local `StripeSubscriptionExpanded` type declares the field as required and the tests hand-built payloads containing it. One refetch per event buys one known shape, pinned by a version number that lives in `package.json` where it can be seen and changed deliberately. It also makes stale deliveries mostly harmless, since a handler writes current truth rather than truth as of emission.

Refetching enables the other half: the **Stripe subscription object is the only writer of subscription state**. Invoice handlers stop writing status and dates from their own reasoning and become resync triggers, which removes the ordering hole where a late `invoice.payment_failed` could stamp `past_due` over a subscription that had already recovered — invoice events were never covered by the ordering guard, which only ever knew about `customer.subscription.*`. The cancel and reactivate endpoints likewise stop writing, calling Stripe and then resyncing, so their HTTP response carries derived truth instead of a local guess. Cancellation eligibility moves to Stripe too: any visitor holding a subscription id may cancel if Stripe reports the subscription live, which is what finally lets someone in dunning stop the retries from the account page.

Emails move from event types to observed state transitions, computed from the before and after that resync already holds. An event that produces no transition sends nothing, which makes duplicate and stale deliveries free, ends the two cancellation emails a single cancel used to generate, and retires the `wasImmediate` flag that was derived from the very field the root-versus-items bug corrupted. Because resync is a read-modify-write with a network call in the middle and Stripe delivers in parallel, it is serialized per subscription with a Postgres advisory lock; without it two concurrent deliveries would each observe the same before-state and each decide the same transition occurred. That lock is the first raw SQL in runtime server code, which is a small pattern cost accepted for an honest before-state.

The dunning path is verified against payloads captured from a real test-clock run — a customer on a clock, a card that fails on renewal, time advanced through failure and recovery, and the resulting events read back from the API and committed as fixtures. This is deliberately short of running events through the deployed handlers: the server holds one Stripe key and one webhook secret, so accepting test-mode events would mean mode-aware key selection on a live payments server plus test rows in the production database, which is more risk and more work than the fix. Fixtures from a real run are enough to close the assumption gap that caused the version bug in the first place, and the live buy-flow already proved the webhook wiring itself. Note that this needs no Stripe sandbox: test mode is built into the live account, so the standing decision to delete `acct_1RwPJK` is unaffected.

A refund or a dispute has to override the paid-through date, because a period that was paid for and then given back is not a period the visitor bought — and a cancelled subscription still reports the period it was cancelled in, so `customer.subscription.deleted` would otherwise restore exactly the entitlement the refund removed. That override is `access_revoked` on the Stripe subscription, read by `deriveSubscriptionState` and by nothing else. Keeping it on the subscription rather than the profile is what makes it survive a resync: profile fields are rewritten wholesale from Stripe on every event, so a flag stored there would be erased by the first resync that followed it. But a single boolean was not enough, and the gap it left was silent and expensive. Stripe does not cancel a subscription because one of its invoices was refunded, so a goodwill refund left the flag set while renewals kept succeeding — the visitor was billed month after month with entitlement pinned to null, and only a won dispute could ever clear it. The flag therefore records **why** access was revoked and **which period** the revoked charge bought. A dispute is money still being contested and is resolved only by `charge.dispute.closed`. A refund covers one period, and is lifted by `invoice.payment_succeeded` once a later period has been paid for — "later" meaning the subscription's current period starts at or after the revoked period's end, which is what distinguishes a genuine renewal from a retry of the refunded invoice, since a retry leaves the subscription sitting inside the period that was refunded. Revocations written before those two keys existed carry neither, and are treated as refunds: a wrongly cleared dispute is put back by its own closing event, whereas a wrongly kept refund locks a paying visitor out permanently and invisibly. `scripts/audit-access-revocations.ts` is the read-only view of who is currently in that state.

Revoking access is not on its own enough, because Stripe keeps billing: a full refund therefore also cancels the subscription (`cancelRefundedSubscription`), written after the flag because Stripe refuses metadata writes on a cancelled subscription, and before the resync so the profile is written once from a subscription already in its final state. A rejection is tolerated only when the subscription turns out to be cancelled already — cancel-then-refund is the ordinary support flow — and any other rejection fails the webhook, since a live subscription that failed to cancel is precisely the bug. An *open* dispute is deliberately excluded: it may still be won, and a cancelled subscription cannot be un-cancelled. A dispute that closes as `lost` is not excluded, and excluding it was the same money bug in a second place — the flag survives, but only `invoice.payment_succeeded` lifts one and it may not lift a dispute, so the subscription renewed forever with entitlement pinned to null and no event left that could recover it. A lost dispute is therefore treated exactly as a refund: money returned, billing stopped, access off. `lost` alone cancels; any other non-restoring closing status is left running for a later event to resolve, since the cancel is the irreversible direction.
