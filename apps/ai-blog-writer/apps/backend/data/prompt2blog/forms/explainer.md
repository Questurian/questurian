## Use when

Use when readers need a clear mental model before they can act or form a view. Suitable subjects include visa systems, transit networks, tourism rules, pricing mechanisms, local customs, environmental events, or unfamiliar industry terms.

## Do not use when

Do not use for breaking developments whose timing is the main value, personal advocacy, or a step-by-step task. Use News Report, Opinion/Column, or How-To/Checklist instead. If the commission asks whether a proposition remains true, use Analysis.

## Reader promise

Answer what the subject is, how it works, why it exists, whom it affects, and where exceptions apply. Define every necessary term before relying on it. Leave readers able to interpret future information without memorizing the article.

## Required evidence

Prefer official rules, primary documentation, subject-matter specialists, and strong explanatory reporting. Verify jurisdiction, eligibility, dates, thresholds, and exceptions. Use examples to illuminate rules, not replace them. When authorities disagree or implementation differs from written policy, label both and explain the evidentiary limit.

## Allowed structures

1. Short answer, key terms, mechanism, example, exceptions, practical meaning.
2. Reader misconception, accurate model, component-by-component explanation, implications.
3. Timeline or process map followed by the rules that govern each stage.

Choose the smallest structure that makes the system coherent. Use questions as subheads only when they match real reader questions.

## Failure modes

Avoid circular definitions, unexplained acronyms, fake simplicity, generic history, and long procedural detours. Do not convert estimates into guarantees or jurisdiction-specific rules into universal advice. Never invent an exception to make the explanation feel complete; mark the gap.

## Headline note

Name the exact concept and reader benefit. “How X works” is useful when mechanism is central. Avoid inflated claims such as “the complete guide” for a bounded explanation.
