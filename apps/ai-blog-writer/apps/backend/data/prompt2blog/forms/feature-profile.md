## Use when

Use when the commission promises character, scene, observation, and reported depth rather than a reference guide. A profile centers a person or group; a feature may center a place, practice, enterprise, or cultural change while still relying on human voices and concrete scenes.

## Do not use when

Do not use when research contains only generic web summaries, no attributable voices, or no observed or documented scenes. Use Explainer or Analysis if evidence is principally documentary. Do not fabricate color to imitate magazine writing.

## Reader promise

Bring readers close to the approved subject while explaining its wider significance. Specific people, moments, places, and quotations should reveal the story. Narrative detail must remain factual and attributed.

## Required evidence

Require supplied interviews, transcripts, field notes, firsthand material, or sufficiently detailed reported sources supporting every promised person, scene, and quotation. Identify who observed each scene and when. Verify biographical facts, chronology, roles, and relevant context independently where possible. Composite characters, invented dialogue, imagined thoughts, reconstructed sensory detail, and unattributed quotations are prohibited.

## Allowed structures

1. Defining scene, subject background, central tension, reported development, resonant return.
2. Profile portrait, formative history, present work, stakes, outside perspective.
3. Thematic feature moving among two or three reported scenes, with context between them.

Narrative structure cannot broaden the commission’s primary subject. Secondary voices provide perspective, not competing profiles, unless explicitly approved.

## Failure modes

Avoid hero worship, promotional biography, decorative quotations, invented interiority, chronology confusion, and scenes assembled from unrelated facts. Do not claim representativeness from one person. If promised access, quotations, or scenes are missing, stop with a research gap.

## Headline note

Prefer the subject’s name or defining tension over generic inspiration language. A feature headline may be evocative, but its deck or opening must quickly establish the factual subject.
