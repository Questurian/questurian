## Comparison Article Guide

### Purpose & Reader Intent

Comparison articles help readers evaluate multiple options—products, services, ideas or methods—by highlighting their similarities and differences. Readers expect a balanced analysis that identifies how the options stack up against specific criteria and offers insight into which choice may suit different needs.

### Core Content Expectations

- **Clear identification of the items:** Explicitly state what is being compared and ensure they are equivalent and truly comparable.
    
- **Stated criteria for comparison:** Define the specific factors (features, performance metrics, price, etc.) that will be used to evaluate each option.
    
- **Structured comparison:** Organize the analysis either by item (block structure) or by criteria (point‑by‑point). Point‑by‑point compares each option under the same criterion one after another, while block style discusses one option in depth before moving to the next. Choose the structure that best serves clarity.
    
- **Objective analysis:** Present strengths and weaknesses of each option with evidence—statistics, user feedback, expert opinions—and avoid bias. Provide context where numbers might need interpretation.
    
- **Implications for the reader:** Explain how differences matter in practice. Offer guidance on which option is suited for specific circumstances, but acknowledge that the best choice depends on individual priorities.
    

### Optional Enhancing Elements

- **Historical or market context:** A brief background on how each option emerged and how they are typically used.
    
- **User scenarios or case studies:** Illustrate how different users might experience each option, which helps readers envision their own use case.
    
- **Comparison tables:** A table comparing features side‑by‑side can enhance comprehension.
    
- **Expert or user quotes:** Credible testimonials can add nuance to the evaluation, provided sources are cited.
    

### Signs the Source Material Is Insufficient

- Does not clearly state the two (or more) items being compared or confuses them with unrelated items.
    
- Fails to specify comparison criteria, resulting in vague or meaningless judgments.
    
- Provides information about only one option or covers each option in isolation without actually comparing them.
    
- Lacks evidence or examples to support claims about strengths and weaknesses.
    
- Omits analysis of how differences impact the reader’s decision, leaving no basis for a recommendation.
    

### Editorial Flexibility Notes

- The tone should be neutral and informative.
    
- The conclusion can either declare a “winner” or emphasize that the best choice depends on individual needs. Both approaches are acceptable provided reasoning is transparent.
