What runs, in order

One trip setup. Then this loop for each day.

Set up the trip

Choose location, day count, timing and preferences. Pick or edit a layout: breakfast, market, lunch, walk, and so on. Approve it.

No model call

This establishes placeholders and their order. It does not find real venues.

Talk through the day

The Grill settles purpose, geography, priorities, stop roles, meals/rest, overlap with other days, allowed changes and unknowns.

AI call per turn

It cannot search or verify venues. It stops when its checklist is covered, not after a fixed question count.

Approve the direction

Another AI call writes the agreement into structured requirements. You accept those requirements; the app builds the research assignment.

One extraction call · assembly is free

This is why a casual “Sounds right” can later become a hard research obligation.

Research and write

The AI chooses an anchor, finds nearby places, reads pages, checks hours and journeys, then writes a title, intro and every stop’s blurb.

One job · many model/tool turns

You can run it in-app or copy the assignment outside. There is no separate mandatory blurb phase after this.

Check and save

The app checks the answer’s structure, matching stop IDs, references and schedule arithmetic. You preview it and save it. Incomplete planning can still be saved.

No model call

It does not independently read the source pages to prove the claims are true.

Review the saved day

Read the day, inspect evidence, record review notes. Planning is marked complete only when required stops, times, journeys and critical checks are resolved.

No automatic repair or publication

Notes and the “I read the sources” checkbox do not change the itinerary or clear planning problems.

Other days’ results and accepted directions help avoid repetition. Changing relevant trip/day context can make an earlier research assignment stale; existing work is retained.

Why it never feels finished

The page keeps the interview, full direction, research prompt, import controls and saved result in the same column. Warnings appear before the finished prose. Even a usable draft therefore looks like an ongoing process.

There is also a real product gap: it can detect and describe problems, but this saved-day view offers review notes rather than direct stop editing or one-click repair. A review note is stored beside the result; it does not fix a time or replace a venue.

One concrete wording bug: the saved result’s top warning says nothing has been checked even though a separate checkbox records that you read the sources. The top warning is unconditional.

What the reduction did—and did not do

Less packaging. Still the same demanding assignment.

The reduction removed repeated interview material from research input, shortened the answer format, consolidated requirements and stopped requesting multiple versions of the same explanation. It did not erase previously accepted requirements.

Audit judgment · proposed, not implemented

Yes: let the AI choose more. Ask you less.

Your proposed approach fits this product: give the AI location, day shape and essential preferences; let it pick a coherent route; show the result. Much of that selection already happens. The extra burden is agreeing too much in advance and exposing every intermediate object afterward.

Keep

  • Real places and exact branches.
  • Basic opening-day checks.
  • A route that fits the available time.
  • Clear optional stops and rest.
  • Sources available on demand.
  • A blurb explaining what to do and why this stop belongs.

Simplify

  • Ask only unresolved decisions that would materially change the day.
  • Offer an “AI decides” path through preferences.
  • Show a short agreement; keep detailed requirements expandable.
  • Put the finished day first after research.
  • Show one concise list of real decisions, with actionable fixes.
  • Move prompts, history, evidence and nonblocking caveats behind disclosures.

A simpler target flow: location + day shape → AI researches and writes → “Here is your day, and here is why” → resolve genuine problems → accept the draft.

For editorial itineraries, state which days the route works instead of implicitly promising every day. Leave unspecified personal needs unspecified; do not turn them into universal venue requirements.

What comes next for the current draft? Review the existing blurbs; decide the itinerary’s operating days; correct the three schedule gaps; revisit the inherited dietary/access requirement. You do not need another general writing phase just to get stop blurbs. A later publishing handoff is separate from this day workspace.

Verdict: the research and schedule checks have value. The amount of visible process is not required for choosing seven good stops. Simplify the agreement and completion experience before removing the checks that catch real failures.
